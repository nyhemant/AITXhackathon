🐟 Manta ray

1

Wings

What do a manta’s broad fins work like?

Wikipedia says mantas move through the water by the wing-like movements of their pectoral fins. Those broad triangular fins work like underwater wings.

2

Horns

What sit beside a manta’s wide forward mouth?

Wikipedia says mantas have horn-shaped cephalic fins beside the mouth. People once called them “devilfish” because those horns looked “evil.” The fins are feeding tools, not weapons.

3

Filter

How do mantas gather their food?

Wikipedia says mantas are filter feeders. They flatten the horn-shaped cephalic fins to channel zooplankton into the open mouth as they swim.

4

Big

How big can a manta get, if we keep the size soft?

Wikipedia lists mantas among the largest rays. Disc width can reach several meters. Exact records shift, so we keep that size soft.

5

Reef and ocean

How do reef mantas and giant oceanic mantas use the sea?

Wikipedia’s soft split: reef mantas tend to stay nearer coasts, while giant oceanic mantas roam farther offshore. Maps and names can shift, so we keep that lifestyle story soft.

6

Live pups

How are baby mantas born?

Wikipedia says gestation lasts over a year and mantas give birth to live pups. Pregnancy length can vary, so we keep that story soft.

7

Cleaning

Why do mantas visit coral cleaning stations?

Wikipedia says mantas visit cleaning stations on coral reefs so cleaner fish can pick off external parasites.

8

Breach

What do mantas sometimes do at the surface — and do we know why?

Wikipedia says mantas may breach or leap out of the water. Possible ideas include a signal or shaking off hitchhikers, but the reason is not known — we keep that story soft.

9

Soft care

How can people help mantas?

Wikipedia ties pressure on mantas to entanglement in fishing nets and demand for their gill plates. Slow breeding makes that pressure harder to bounce back from. Soft care is keeping warm seas healthy — we do not lock one status letter.

10

Myth buster

Do a manta’s horns mean it is dangerous?

Wikipedia says people once feared mantas as “devilfish,” but divers found them placid and safe. The horn fins help funnel plankton. Mantas are gentle filter-feeders, not man-eaters.
