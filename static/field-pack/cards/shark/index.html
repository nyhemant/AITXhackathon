Quick tips (Junior Ranger) — tap to open
  • Sharks have a skeleton made of cartilage, not bone.
  • Skin has tiny tooth-like scales (dermal denticles) and can feel rough.
  • Most sharks live in the ocean and eat meat or fish.
  • They breathe with a row of gill slits on each side of the head.
  • Most kinds of sharks are not dangerous to people.
1

Cartilage skeleton

What are a shark’s “bones” made of?

Sharks have a skeleton made of cartilage, not bone. Cartilage is strong but bendy — not the hard bone in a dog or a person.

2

Sandpaper skin

Why can shark skin feel rough, like sandpaper?

Shark skin is covered in tiny tooth-like scales called dermal denticles. They can make the skin feel rough.

3

Ocean home

Where do most sharks live?

Sharks live in all the world’s seas. A few kinds can enter rivers, but most do not live in fresh water.

4

Meat or fish

What do most sharks eat?

Most sharks eat meat or fish. A few kinds filter tiny food from the water, so diets differ by kind.

5

Gill slits

How do sharks breathe?

Sharks breathe with gills. They have a row of gill slits on each side of the head — often about five to seven.

6

Swishy swimmers

How is a shark’s body built for moving?

Wikipedia describes sharks as strong swimmers with a powerful body and fins. The Field Trip Kit card calls that a strong swishy body.

7

New teeth

What happens to a shark’s teeth over time?

Sharks grow teeth in the gums and replace them again and again throughout life.

8

Electric sense

What extra sense can sharks use to find other animals?

Sharks can sense the weak electric fields made by other animals. How those tiny sensors work comes later.

9

Soft care

How are sharks doing in the wild today?

Fishing and finning can hurt many kinds of sharks. How each kind is doing is different — we do not lock one status letter.

10

Myth buster

Are most sharks the movie “man-eaters” people imagine?

Movies make sharks look like man-eaters, but of hundreds of kinds, most are not dangerous to people.

Facts from Wikipedia, Shark. Where sources disagree on exact numbers, we keep them approximate.
